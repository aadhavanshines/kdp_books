# Chapter 2: Why Most AI Pilots Never Pay Back

Surveys of large companies keep finding the same thing: most organizations are experimenting with AI, and only a small share can point to a measurable effect on their profit. The exact percentages vary from survey to survey and year to year, but the shape does not. Lots of pilots. Few results.

The reason is rarely the technology. The models available today are good enough for a large range of business work. Pilots fail for business reasons, and the same seven reasons come up so often that you can use them as a checklist. If you can see one of them in a project you are about to fund, fix it first.

## Failure 1: No Baseline

A pilot that does not measure the "before" cannot prove the "after." This is the most common failure and the easiest to prevent.

A team runs an AI pilot for customer email. At the end, everyone agrees it "felt faster." But nobody recorded how long replies took before the pilot, how many emails each agent handled, or how many customers wrote back a second time because the first answer was incomplete. The pilot ends with opinions instead of numbers, and opinions do not survive a budget meeting.

**The fix:** before any pilot starts, write down three to five numbers that describe today's process, measured the same way you will measure the pilot. Two to four weeks of baseline data is usually enough. Chapter 17 shows what to measure.

## Failure 2: A Solution Looking for a Problem

Someone sees an impressive demo and asks, "Where could we use this?" The answer is usually "everywhere, a little," which in practice means nowhere, a lot.

Good AI projects start the other way round: from a business problem that already has an owner, a cost and a metric. "Quote turnaround is 26 hours and we lose deals because of it" is a problem. "We should use AI agents" is not.

**The fix:** every use case must name the business problem, the person who owns it, and the number that will change. If nobody owns the problem, nobody will own the result.

## Failure 3: Licenses Without Change

Buying AI licenses for everyone feels like progress. Usage rises for a few weeks, then settles at a level where a minority use the tool daily and most use it rarely. Nobody's job has changed, so nobody's results have changed.

This is not a reason to avoid giving people access. General AI assistants do raise individual productivity, and broad access builds the skills and ideas you need later. But a license is not a use case. Value comes when a process is redesigned around the tool: a different way of answering email, a different way of preparing a proposal, a different review step.

**The fix:** treat general licenses as training and discovery, budgeted accordingly. Expect the measurable returns from a small number of redesigned processes.

## Failure 4: Ignoring the 80 Percent That Isn't Technology

In most successful AI projects, the technology is the smaller part of the work. The larger part is people and process: redesigning the steps, writing the instructions, training staff, changing targets, handling fear about jobs, and deciding who checks what.

A typical failed pilot spends three months on the technical setup and three days on training. Staff are handed a tool, told it will help, and left to work out how. Some love it, most ignore it, and a few find ways to make it look bad because they are worried about what it means for them.

**The fix:** in your project plan, budget at least as much time and money for process redesign, training and communication as for technology. Chapter 16 covers the people side in detail.

## Failure 5: Data That Isn't Ready

AI that answers questions about your products is only as good as your product data. AI that drafts replies about orders needs to see the order system. AI that reviews contracts needs the contracts in a readable form, not as scanned images in a shared drive nobody has organized since 2015.

Many pilots stall in week three when the team discovers that the information the AI needs is incomplete, out of date, spread across systems, or locked behind permissions nobody can approve.

**The fix:** before scoring a use case as "feasible," ask exactly what information it needs, where that information lives, and who can grant access. Chapter 5 builds this into the scoring.

## Failure 6: Pilot Purgatory

Some pilots succeed and still never scale. The pilot worked for 12 enthusiastic people in one branch. Then nobody set aside budget for the rollout, the integration work was never finished, the champion moved to another job, and the pilot quietly ran on in one corner of the company for two years.

Pilot purgatory is expensive because it uses up the best people's goodwill and produces nothing that shows up in the accounts.

**The fix:** decide at the start what result would trigger scaling, what scaling would cost, and who would pay for it. Put a date on the decision. A pilot must end with one of three outcomes: scale, fix and re-test, or stop.

## Failure 7: Risk Discovered Too Late

A pilot goes well. Then, two weeks before launch, someone from legal or IT security sees it for the first time and asks where the customer data is processed, whether the vendor keeps it, and what happens if the AI gives a customer wrong information about a refund. The launch is delayed for months, or cancelled.

**The fix:** involve risk, legal and IT at the start, in a light way. Most AI use cases fall into a low-risk tier that needs only a short checklist. A few need more care. Chapter 15 shows how to sort them quickly so that governance speeds projects up instead of stopping them at the end.

## The Pattern Behind the Failures

Look at the seven failures together and a pattern appears. Every one of them is a management failure, not a technical one:

| Failure | What was missing |
| --- | --- |
| No baseline | Measurement |
| Solution looking for a problem | A business owner |
| Licenses without change | Process redesign |
| Ignoring people and process | Change management |
| Data not ready | Honest feasibility check |
| Pilot purgatory | A decision rule and a scaling budget |
| Risk found too late | Early, light governance |

That is good news for you as a leader, because these are exactly the things leaders control. You cannot make a model more accurate. You can insist on a baseline, an owner, a decision date and a budget for the people side.

## Northfield's First Attempt

Northfield Supply had, in fact, already run an AI pilot before Dana's board asked for a plan. Eighteen months earlier, the IT manager had bought 50 licenses for a general AI assistant and given them to "people who seemed interested." There was no training beyond a 30-minute webinar, no target and no baseline.

When Dana's team reviewed it, they found that 14 of the 50 people used the tool at least weekly. Several of them were enthusiastic: a branch manager used it to write staff schedules, a buyer used it to compare supplier terms, and two people in customer service used it to draft replies. But none of this had been shared, measured or built into any process. The company had spent about $18,000 a year and could not say what it had received.

Marcus, the CFO, used this as evidence that AI was overhyped. Dana drew a different conclusion. The 14 regular users had found real value on their own, with no help. The pilot had not failed because AI was useless. It had failed because nobody managed it. The question for the new plan was how to turn those scattered personal wins into measured business results.

She kept the 14 users and made them the core of a small group of AI champions (Chapter 16), and she asked Priya, whose customer service team had the clearest problem and the clearest numbers, to sponsor the first real pilot.

## A Pre-Funding Checklist

Before you approve any AI pilot, ask for a single page that answers these questions. If the answers are vague, the pilot is not ready.

1. What business problem does this solve, and who owns it?
2. What are today's numbers (the baseline), and how were they measured?
3. What will the numbers need to be at the end of the pilot for us to scale it?
4. Which of the four kinds of value (cost, revenue, speed, risk) does it create?
5. What data does it need, where does that data live, and who has approved access?
6. Who checks the AI's output, how, and what happens when it is wrong?
7. What is the risk tier, and have legal, security and IT seen it?
8. What does the pilot cost, and what would scaling cost?
9. When is the decision date, and who decides?
10. How much time is budgeted for training and process change?

## Key Points

- Most AI pilots fail for management reasons: no baseline, no owner, no process change, unready data, no decision rule, and risk found too late.
- Start from a business problem with an owner and a number, not from a technology looking for a use.
- General licenses build skills and ideas. Measurable returns come from redesigned processes.
- Budget at least as much for people and process as for technology.
- Every pilot ends with a decision: scale, fix and re-test, or stop.

## Questions for Your Leadership Team

1. Which AI pilots or tools have we already paid for? What can we prove they delivered?
2. Which of the seven failures are we most likely to repeat? Be honest.
3. Who in our organization is already getting value from AI on their own? How would we find out?
4. Do we have a standard way to approve and end projects? Does it work for AI, or does AI need something lighter?
5. What would it take for our finance team to trust an AI business case?
