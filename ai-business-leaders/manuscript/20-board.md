# Chapter 20: The Board and AI

Boards are under pressure on AI from two directions. Investors, lenders and the press ask whether the company is moving fast enough. Regulators, auditors and insurers ask whether it is moving safely. Directors are expected to oversee both, often with little personal experience of the technology.

This chapter is for board members and for the executives who report to them. It covers what a board's role on AI is, what to ask management, how to report AI to the board, and how directors can build their own understanding.

## The Board's Role

The board's job on AI is the same as its job on any major strategic issue: to make sure management has a sensible strategy, is executing it, and is managing the risks. Boards should not choose vendors or approve individual use cases. They should be confident that the company has a process that does those things well.

In practice, a board should be able to answer four questions at any time:

1. **Strategy:** How does AI affect our market, our competitors and our business model, and what is our response?
2. **Value:** What has AI actually delivered so far, measured honestly against what we spent?
3. **Risk:** What are the main AI risks for us, and how are they governed?
4. **Capability:** Do we have the leadership, skills and data to do this well?

## Questions Directors Should Ask

Here are questions that cut through slides and enthusiasm. A well-run AI program will answer them easily. A poorly run one will struggle.

**On strategy**

- Where could AI change how customers buy from us, or how competitors compete with us, in the next three years?
- Which parts of our business are most exposed: where could a competitor using AI offer the same thing much cheaper or faster?
- Are we treating AI mainly as a way to cut costs, or also as a way to grow? Why?

**On value**

- Which AI use cases are in production, and what has each delivered against its business case?
- How do you measure results, and would our auditors accept the method?
- What have we stopped, and why?
- What are we spending on AI in total, including licenses, projects and people?

**On risk**

- Do we have a register of every AI use case we run? Can we see it?
- Which uses are high-risk, and how are they approved and monitored?
- What AI incidents have we had, including minor ones? What did we change?
- How are we protecting customer and employee data in AI tools?
- What regulations apply to our AI use, and who tracks changes?
- How would we detect AI-powered fraud, such as a fake voice or video of an executive?

**On capability and people**

- Who owns AI at executive level? Is it a business leader or only IT?
- What are we telling employees about AI and jobs? Is it consistent with our plans?
- How many of our executives use AI themselves for real work?

> **Tip:** Ask to see the AI register (Chapter 15). If management can produce a current list of every AI use case, with an owner, a risk tier and results, governance is probably in decent shape. If it takes weeks to compile, it probably isn't.

## How Management Should Report AI to the Board

A quarterly one-page update is usually enough for a mid-sized company. Appendix A has a template (Template 8). It should cover:

1. Progress and decisions this quarter.
2. The portfolio: use cases in production and pilot, with status.
3. Results against business cases: benefits and costs to date.
4. Risks and incidents, and changes made.
5. Regulatory developments and the company's response.
6. People: adoption, training and employee sentiment.
7. Next quarter's plans and any decisions needed from the board.

Two principles make board reporting credible:

- **Report misses as clearly as successes.** A board that only ever hears good news stops trusting the report.
- **Use the same numbers every quarter.** Changing metrics from quarter to quarter makes progress impossible to judge.

At Northfield, Dana's first AI board update was a single page with the AI register attached. The board's response surprised her. The directors were less interested in the customer email results than in the governance: who approved what, what happened when something went wrong, and what employees had been told. One director, who chaired another company's audit committee, said it was the first AI report she had seen that she could actually oversee.

## Where AI Sits in Board Structure

Boards handle AI oversight in different ways. The common options:

- **Full board.** AI strategy and major risks are discussed by the whole board at least twice a year. Suits most mid-sized companies.
- **Audit or risk committee.** Governance, controls, incidents and regulatory compliance are reviewed in detail by the committee that already oversees risk. Often combined with full-board strategy discussions.
- **Technology committee.** Some larger companies have one; it can take AI as part of its remit.

Whichever you choose, make it explicit. "Everyone and no one" is the default, and it means no one.

## Building Directors' Own Understanding

Directors do not need to become technical. But a board where no one has used AI will struggle to judge management's claims. Practical steps:

- **Personal use.** Encourage directors to use an AI assistant for a month, in the way described in Chapter 3, on non-confidential tasks.
- **A hands-on session.** A two-hour session where management shows real use cases, including failures, and directors try the tools themselves.
- **Outside views.** Occasional briefings from someone independent of management and vendors.
- **Board use of AI.** If directors want to use AI to read board papers, agree the rules first. Board papers are among the most confidential documents in the company.

## Avoiding Two Board Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Pushing for speed without asking for evidence.** A board that tells management to "move faster on AI" without asking what has worked encourages exactly the scattered pilots and license spending described in Chapter 2. Ask for proof, then fund scale.

**Mistake 2: Treating AI only as a risk.** A board that only asks about risks encourages management to do nothing visible. Doing nothing is also a risk, if competitors are improving their costs and service. Ask about both.

## Key Points

- The board's role is to oversee AI strategy, value, risk and capability, not to choose tools or approve use cases.
- Directors should ask specific questions about results, the AI register, incidents, data, regulation and people.
- A quarterly one-page report with the AI register attached is usually enough for a mid-sized company.
- Decide explicitly where AI oversight sits: full board, audit or risk committee, or a technology committee.
- Directors should build personal experience with AI and agree rules before using it on board papers.
- Avoid both pushing for speed without evidence and treating AI only as a risk.
