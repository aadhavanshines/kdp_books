# Introduction

Most companies have already "done something with AI." They bought licenses for a chat assistant. They ran a pilot or two. Someone gave a demo at an all-hands meeting that drew a round of applause. Then, a year later, the finance team asked a simple question: what did we get for the money? And nobody could answer it.

This book is about answering that question before anyone has to ask it.

It is written for the people who decide where AI goes in an organization and who answer for the results: chief executives, general managers, department heads, business owners, board members, and the managers who will be handed "the AI project" next quarter. You do not need a technical background. You will not write any code. You do need to make decisions, and this book gives you a way to make them well.

## The Rule in the Title

The whole book rests on one rule: **prove that an AI use case pays back on a small scale before you spend money to scale it.**

That sounds obvious. Almost nobody does it. The usual pattern runs the other way. A company buys an enterprise license for everyone, hopes that people find good uses, and measures success by how many people logged in. Or it funds a large, custom project on the strength of a vendor demo, with no baseline to measure against and no agreed point at which to stop.

Proof before scale means something specific:

1. You pick a small number of use cases where the value can be measured in money, time or risk.
2. You write down the baseline, the target and the cost before you start.
3. You run a pilot with real users and real work for a fixed period, usually six to eight weeks.
4. You decide, on the numbers, to scale it, fix it or stop it.
5. Only the use cases that pass get the bigger budget.

This is not a slow approach. A disciplined company can run its first proven pilot in 90 days and have three or four in production within a year. What it avoids is the expensive kind of slow: two years of scattered experiments that never add up to anything a finance director would recognize.

## What Makes This Book Different

I read the popular books on AI for leaders before writing this one, and studied what their readers said about them. The same complaints came up again and again. Too general. Too basic. No real numbers. Examples that dated within a year. Advice that could apply to any change program. Books that seemed to exist mainly to sell the author's consulting services.

This book tries to fix each of those problems:

- **Real numbers.** Chapter 6 builds a full three-year business case, line by line, with the costs most people forget. You can copy the structure for your own projects.
- **Decisions, not descriptions.** Every chapter ends with the questions your leadership team should discuss and the decisions you need to make.
- **Every function.** Part III goes through sales, marketing, customer service, finance, operations, HR, legal and IT, with the use cases that tend to pay back, the metrics to track and the traps to avoid.
- **Templates you can use tomorrow.** Appendix A contains eight templates, including a use-case canvas, a business case, a vendor scorecard, a pilot charter, a one-page AI use policy and a board update. Appendix B adds 30 ready-to-use prompts for leaders, and the paperback also has fill-in worksheets.
- **Vendor-neutral.** I mention well-known products only as examples. Nothing in this book depends on which AI vendor you choose, and nothing in it is for sale.
- **Built to last.** AI products change every few months. The way to choose, fund, govern and measure them does not. Where a fact might change, I tell you how to check it.

## A Company to Follow

To keep the numbers concrete, the book follows one fictional company from first idea to scaled program. **Northfield Supply** is a regional distributor of building and plumbing materials. It has 620 employees, 14 branches, about $210 million in annual revenue, and thin margins, like most distributors. Its chief executive, Dana Reyes, has been told by her board to "have an AI plan by the spring." Her chief financial officer, Marcus Hale, is skeptical. Her head of customer service, Priya Nair, has a team that is drowning in email.

You will see Northfield make good decisions and bad ones. You will see its first business case, its vendor choice, its pilot results and its plan for year two. The company and its people are invented, but every problem it meets is common, and the numbers are typical of a business its size.

Smaller organizations are not left out. Where the approach changes for a company of 20 or 50 people, I say so. The principles are the same at every size; only the scale of the spreadsheet changes.

## How to Read This Book

The book has four parts:

- **Part I: The Leader's View** (Chapters 1 to 3) explains what AI can and cannot do in business terms, why most AI pilots fail to pay back, and how to build your own AI habit so that you lead from experience instead of from slides.
- **Part II: Choosing and Funding** (Chapters 4 to 7) shows how to find use cases, score them, build an honest business case and choose between building, buying and configuring.
- **Part III: AI Across Every Function** (Chapters 8 to 14) goes department by department through the use cases that work, then gives quick-start lists for ten industries.
- **Part IV: Governing and Scaling** (Chapters 15 to 21) covers governance without bureaucracy, people and change, measurement, the 90-day plan, how to scale from pilots to an AI-ready company, what boards should ask, and honest answers to the objections you will hear.

If you have a pressing decision, go straight to the chapter you need. If you are starting from nothing, read Parts I and II first, then use Part III as a reference and Part IV as your plan.

> **Tip:** Read this book with your leadership team. Each chapter ends with "Questions for Your Leadership Team." Taking one chapter per meeting is an easy way to build a shared view, and a shared view is worth more than any single strategy document.

## A Word on Honesty

AI is useful and it is overhyped, both at the same time. Some of what you have heard is true. Much of it is sales talk. In this book I try to tell you plainly where AI pays back today, where it might pay back soon, and where you should wait. When I give a number, I tell you where it came from or that it is an example. When a decision depends on your situation, I say so instead of pretending there is one right answer.
