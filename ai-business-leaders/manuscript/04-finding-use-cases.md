# Part II: Choosing and Funding

# Chapter 4: Finding the Right Use Cases

The best AI use cases in your company are probably not the ones in the vendor brochures. They are hidden in the daily work of your teams: the inbox that never empties, the report that takes two days every month, the spreadsheet that one person updates by hand, the question customers ask 300 times a week.

This chapter gives you a simple method to find them. It takes about two weeks and needs no consultants.

## Where Value Hides

AI use cases that pay back tend to share five features. Use them as a lens when you look at any process.

1. **High volume.** The task happens hundreds or thousands of times a month. Small savings on each one add up.
2. **Language-heavy.** The work is mostly reading, writing, summarizing, sorting or answering. That is what generative AI does best.
3. **Rules-based but messy.** There is a right answer, or a clear policy, but the inputs arrive in many forms: emails, PDFs, phone notes, scanned documents.
4. **Checkable.** A person can tell quickly whether the output is right. Checking a draft takes a fraction of the time it takes to write one.
5. **Costly when slow or wrong.** Delays lose sales or annoy customers; errors cost money or create risk.

A task with all five is a strong candidate. A task with only one or two is usually not worth the effort yet.

## The Opportunity Map

The opportunity map is a short exercise you run with each department. The goal is a long list of candidate use cases, described in business terms.

### Step 1: Ask the Right Questions

Meet each department head and two or three people who do the work every day. People closest to the work know where the time goes. Ask:

- What do you spend the most hours on each week?
- What do you do over and over that feels like it should be easier?
- What do customers or colleagues wait for from you?
- Where do mistakes happen, and what do they cost?
- What information do you look up most often, and how long does it take to find?
- If you had an extra person for a month, what would you give them?

### Step 2: Look at the Data You Already Have

Interviews tell you what people feel. Data tells you what is happening. Ask for:

- Volumes: emails, calls, tickets, invoices, orders, quotes, applications per week.
- Times: average handling time, turnaround time, time to close the month.
- Backlogs and overtime: where work piles up, and where you pay extra to clear it.
- Error and rework rates: returns, credit notes, corrections, complaints.

### Step 3: Write Each Candidate in One Sentence

Use this format:

> **Note:** "For [team], AI could [do what] so that [business result], measured by [metric]."

For example: "For customer service, AI could draft replies to order-status and delivery emails so that agents answer faster and we avoid hiring for growth, measured by handling time per email and response time."

If you cannot complete the sentence, the idea is not ready to score.

### Step 4: Ask AI to Help

Your AI assistant is useful for this exercise. Describe a department and ask it to suggest use cases you may have missed. Treat the result as a prompt for discussion, not a list to adopt.

```
I run a regional building and plumbing supplies distributor with 620
employees and 14 branches. Our finance team of 22 handles accounts
payable (about 6,000 supplier invoices a month in many formats),
accounts receivable, credit control and month-end close. List ten
ways generative AI could help this team, each in one sentence using
this format: "AI could [do what] so that [result], measured by
[metric]." Mark each as high, medium or low volume.
```

## Northfield's Opportunity Map

After two weeks of interviews and data requests, Northfield's small working group (Priya from customer service, a finance manager, the sales operations lead, an IT architect and two of the AI champions) had 31 candidates. They merged duplicates and dropped the vague ones, leaving twelve:

| # | Department | Candidate use case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Customer service | Draft replies to order-status, delivery and stock emails |
| 2 | Sales | Draft quotes from customer emails and drawings, for review by sales staff |
| 3 | Finance | Extract data from supplier invoices in any format into the payables system |
| 4 | Sales | Summarize each customer's history before account reviews |
| 5 | Customer service | Answer branch staff questions about product specs from manufacturer data |
| 6 | Marketing | Write and update product descriptions for the online catalog (18,000 items) |
| 7 | HR | Answer employee questions about policies and benefits |
| 8 | Operations | Predict stock needs per branch for seasonal products |
| 9 | Finance | Draft first-pass monthly management commentary from the accounts |
| 10 | Credit control | Draft collection emails tailored to each customer's history |
| 11 | Legal | Review supplier contracts for non-standard terms |
| 12 | Operations | AI agent to rebook deliveries automatically when a truck is delayed |

Notice what kind of list this is. It is not "use AI in sales." Each line names a team, a task and, in the working notes behind it, a metric. That makes the next step, scoring, possible.

## Personal Productivity Versus Process Change

As you collect ideas, sort them into two piles.

**Personal productivity** use cases help one person do their own work faster: drafting emails, summarizing documents, preparing for meetings, writing a first version of a report. They are valuable, low-risk and easy to start. They are also hard to measure and rarely show up in the accounts. A general AI assistant plus training covers most of them.

**Process** use cases change how a team or a workflow runs: every customer email gets a drafted reply; every invoice is read by AI before it reaches the payables system; every quote starts from an AI draft. These need more setup, usually some connection to your systems, and a clear owner. They are also where measurable returns come from.

A healthy AI plan has both. Broad access to an assistant builds skills and surfaces ideas. A small portfolio of process use cases delivers the returns that pay for everything. Northfield's twelve candidates were all process use cases; the general assistant rollout ran alongside them as a separate, simpler program.

## A Note for Smaller Companies

If you have 20 or 50 people, you do not need a working group or a two-week exercise. One meeting is enough. Ask each person to bring the one task they would most like help with and how many hours a week it takes. Write the answers on a whiteboard. In most small companies, two or three items will stand out immediately: answering customer email, writing proposals and quotes, and bookkeeping preparation are the usual winners. Pick one and go to Chapter 5.

## Common Mistakes When Finding Use Cases

- **Only asking managers.** Managers know where the problems are reported. Front-line staff know where the time goes. Ask both.
- **Letting vendors drive the list.** Vendors will suggest what they sell. Build your list first, then see who sells what fits.
- **Starting with the most exciting idea.** Agents that run whole processes on their own are exciting. They are rarely the best first project. Save them for when you have experience.
- **Ignoring boring work.** Invoice processing and email replies are dull. They are also high-volume, measurable and low-risk, which is exactly what a first project needs.

## Key Points

- Strong use cases are high-volume, language-heavy, rules-based but messy, checkable and costly when slow or wrong.
- Build an opportunity map by interviewing department heads and front-line staff, and by looking at volumes, times, backlogs and errors.
- Write every candidate as one sentence: team, task, business result, metric.
- Separate personal productivity (broad, easy, hard to measure) from process change (focused, measurable, where returns come from).
- Boring, high-volume work usually makes the best first project.

## Questions for Your Leadership Team

1. Which three processes in our company have the highest volume of language-heavy work?
2. Where do customers wait longest for us, and what does that delay cost?
3. Who should be in our working group, and who are the front-line people we must hear from?
4. Which candidate ideas have come from vendors rather than from our own teams?
5. What is our plan for personal productivity, separate from process use cases?
