# Part I: The Leader's View

# Chapter 1: What Leaders Actually Need to Know About AI

You do not need to understand how an engine works to run a trucking company. You do need to know what a truck can carry, how far it can go on a tank, what it costs per mile, and what happens when a driver falls asleep. This chapter gives you the equivalent for AI: what it can carry, what it costs, and where it breaks.

## Three Kinds of AI You Will Be Sold

When vendors, consultants and your own staff say "AI," they usually mean one of three things. They behave differently, cost differently and fail differently, so it helps to know which one is on the table.

**1. Predictive AI (machine learning).** This is the older, quieter kind. It learns patterns from your historical data and makes a prediction: which customers are likely to leave, how much stock to order, which invoices look like fraud, when a machine is likely to fail. It has been in use for twenty years in banks, retailers and logistics companies. It needs good historical data and usually a data specialist to build and maintain it. When it works, it is very reliable within the range of data it was trained on.

**2. Generative AI.** This is the kind that arrived for most people with ChatGPT at the end of 2022. It produces new content: text, summaries, answers, code, images. The tools behind it are called large language models. They are trained on huge amounts of text, and they predict, word by word, what a good answer to your request looks like. They need no training on your data to be useful, which is why they spread so fast. They are very good with language and very confident, including when they are wrong.

**3. AI agents.** An agent is a generative AI model that can take actions, not just write text. It can look up an order in your system, draft a reply, check a policy, and then send the reply or hand it to a person. Agents can work through several steps on their own. They promise the biggest gains and carry the most risk, because a mistake is no longer a bad paragraph on a screen; it is a wrong refund, a wrong order or a wrong email to a customer.

Most of the opportunity for most companies today sits in the second and third groups, so that is where this book spends most of its time. But keep the first in mind. Some of the best returns in operations and finance still come from plain predictive models, and a good vendor will tell you when an old technique fits better than a new one.

## What Generative AI Is Good At

Think of a generative AI assistant as a very fast, very well-read new hire who has never worked at your company. It is good at:

- **Drafting.** Emails, proposals, job descriptions, policies, reports, product descriptions. A good first draft in seconds, which a person then checks and improves.
- **Summarizing.** Long documents, meeting transcripts, contracts, customer feedback, research. It turns 60 pages into one page you can act on.
- **Answering questions from your own documents.** Given your policies, manuals or product data, it can answer "What is our returns policy for special orders?" in plain language, and point to the source.
- **Sorting and extracting.** Reading 5,000 customer comments and grouping them by theme. Pulling the invoice number, date and amount out of a messy email.
- **Translating and rewriting.** Between languages, and between levels of complexity: "Explain this contract clause to a customer."
- **Analyzing.** Working through a spreadsheet, spotting trends, building a first-pass model, as long as someone checks the result.
- **Brainstorming and challenging.** Generating options, playing devil's advocate, stress-testing a plan.

What these have in common is that they are language-heavy and the work can be checked. That combination is where generative AI pays back most reliably.

## Where It Breaks

Every leader should know these five weaknesses by heart.

**It makes things up.** The industry term is "hallucination." When a model doesn't know an answer, it may produce a plausible one anyway, complete with invented figures, names or legal citations. This happens less than it did in 2023, and much less when the model is given your documents to work from, but it still happens. Any process that uses AI must assume some answers will be wrong and decide who catches them.

**It does not know your business unless you tell it.** A general model knows nothing about your prices, your customers, your policies or what happened last week. It must be connected to your information, or given it in the request, before it can help with company-specific work.

**It can be inconsistent.** Ask the same question twice and you may get two different answers. For creative work that is fine. For a compliance answer it is a problem, and it must be designed out with clear instructions, fixed source documents and checks.

**It can leak or misuse data if you let it.** Free consumer tools may keep what you type and, depending on the settings, use it to improve their models. Business versions usually do not, but you must check the contract. The bigger everyday risk is staff pasting customer data, contracts or financial results into whatever tool is nearest.

**It is weak at judgment and accountability.** AI can list the options for closing a branch. It cannot own the decision, sense the politics, or face the staff. Anything that needs accountability, empathy or a judgment call with real consequences stays with people.

> **Remember:** The question is never "Is the AI accurate?" It is "Is the whole process, AI plus people plus checks, more accurate, faster or cheaper than the process we have today?" A model that is right 90 percent of the time can be very valuable if the other 10 percent is caught cheaply. A model that is right 99 percent of the time can be dangerous if nobody checks the 1 percent.

## How AI Is Priced

Leaders are often surprised by AI costs, both on the low side and the high side. You will meet four pricing models.

**Per-seat licenses.** A monthly fee per user for an assistant built into tools your staff already use, or a standalone chat assistant. Business plans typically cost in the range of $20 to $60 per user per month at the time of writing. This is simple to budget and easy to waste: many companies pay for seats that are rarely used.

**Usage-based (per token).** When your systems call an AI model directly, you pay for the amount of text going in and out, measured in units called tokens (roughly three-quarters of a word each). Prices have fallen sharply every year. For most business tasks, the model itself costs fractions of a cent per request. Costs grow when volumes are large, when long documents are sent with every request, or when agents make many calls to complete one task.

**Per-outcome.** Some vendors now charge per resolved customer conversation, per processed invoice or per qualified lead. This lines the vendor's interest up with yours, but read the definition of "resolved" carefully.

**Platform and project fees.** Software platforms with AI features built in, plus the cost of setting them up, connecting them to your systems and running them. This is usually where most of the money goes.

The model is rarely the expensive part. The expensive parts are connecting AI to your data and systems, changing the process around it, training people, and checking the output. Chapter 6 shows how to put all of these into a business case so none of them comes as a surprise.

## The Four Ways AI Creates Value

Every AI use case that pays back does so in one or more of four ways. Learn to ask which one a proposal claims.

1. **Cost.** The same work is done with fewer hours, less overtime, fewer external contractors, or without hiring for growth.
2. **Revenue.** More sales, higher prices, better conversion, fewer lost customers, faster response to quotes.
3. **Speed.** Work that took days takes hours. Faster quotes, faster month-end close, faster hiring. Speed is valuable when it changes a business result; otherwise it is only nice.
4. **Risk and quality.** Fewer errors, better compliance, fewer missed contract terms, earlier warning of fraud or failure.

A proposal that cannot say which of these it delivers, and roughly how much, is not ready for funding.

> **Warning:** "Time saved" is not money saved. If AI saves each employee 30 minutes a day but nobody does anything different with those 30 minutes, the company's costs do not change. Time saved becomes money only when it avoids a hire, cuts overtime or contractors, or moves people to work that earns more. Chapter 6 shows how to count it honestly.

## What This Means for You as a Leader

You do not have to become an AI expert. You do have to be able to do five things:

- Tell the three kinds of AI apart and ask which one a proposal uses.
- Ask how errors will be caught and who is accountable for them.
- Ask where the data comes from and where it goes.
- Ask which of the four kinds of value a project creates, and how it will be measured.
- Use AI yourself, regularly, so that your judgment rests on experience. Chapter 3 shows how.

At Northfield Supply, Dana Reyes started with exactly this list. In her first leadership meeting on the subject, she asked each department head to bring one example of work that was language-heavy, high-volume and checkable. Priya, in customer service, brought 4,200 emails a week. Marcus, in finance, brought supplier invoices that arrived in 30 different formats. The sales director brought quotes that took an average of 26 hours to send. Those three examples, not a vendor demo, became the starting point for Northfield's AI plan.

## Key Points

- AI comes in three main kinds: predictive, generative and agents. Each costs, works and fails differently.
- Generative AI pays back best on work that is language-heavy, high-volume and easy to check.
- It makes things up, does not know your business unless told, and can be inconsistent. Design every process to catch its mistakes.
- The model is usually cheap. Integration, process change, training and checking are where the money goes.
- AI creates value through cost, revenue, speed, or risk and quality. Time saved only becomes money if something changes.

## Questions for Your Leadership Team

1. Where in our business do people spend the most hours reading, writing, sorting or answering the same kinds of questions?
2. What AI tools are our people already using today, with or without permission?
3. Which of our current processes would be hurt most by a confident wrong answer? Which could tolerate one if it was caught?
4. Of the four kinds of value, which matters most to our strategy this year?
5. Who on this team has used an AI assistant for real work in the last week? What did they learn?
