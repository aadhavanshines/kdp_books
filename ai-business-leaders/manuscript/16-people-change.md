# Chapter 16: People, Skills and Change

Chapter 6 showed that Northfield's business case depended far more on adoption than on cost. That is true for almost every AI project. The technology can be perfect and the project can still fail if people don't use it, use it badly, or quietly resist it because they fear for their jobs.

This chapter is about the people side: what your employees are worried about, how to talk about jobs honestly, how to build skills, and how to get the adoption your business case depends on.

## What People Are Actually Worried About

When you announce an AI program, your employees will hear one question underneath everything you say: "Will I lose my job?" Until you answer that, honestly, nothing else you say will be fully heard.

Other worries follow close behind:

- "Will I be able to learn this?"
- "Will I be blamed when the AI makes a mistake?"
- "Will my work become boring, just checking what a machine wrote?"
- "Will I be judged against colleagues who are better at using it?"
- "Is management using this to watch me?"

These are reasonable concerns. Leaders who dismiss them lose trust. Leaders who address them directly earn the cooperation the project needs.

## Talking About Jobs Honestly

You probably can't promise that AI will never affect any job in your company. Don't. Employees know when a promise is too good to be true, and broken promises are remembered for years.

What you can usually say honestly is something like this:

- What you are trying to achieve (growth without the same growth in hiring; better service; less tedious work).
- How you will handle effects on jobs (absorbing growth first; natural turnover; retraining and redeployment before any reduction).
- What you will not do (use AI to monitor individuals; make decisions about people automatically).
- When people will hear about changes (before they happen, not after).

Here is what Dana said to Northfield's staff when the customer service pilot was announced:

> "We're testing AI to help our customer service team answer routine emails faster. Our business is growing, and without help we'd need to hire several more people next year just to keep up. If this works, we'll hire fewer new people than planned, and the team we have will spend less time on repetitive emails and more on the customers who need real help. We're not planning to reduce the current team because of this pilot. If that ever changes, you'll hear it from me first, with plenty of notice, and with real support for anyone affected."

Notice what she did and did not say. She didn't promise jobs forever. She explained the goal, the plan, what wasn't planned, and how people would be treated. That is the standard to aim for.

## Redesigning Jobs, Not Just Tasks

When AI takes over part of a job, the rest of the job changes. If you don't redesign it deliberately, people end up spending their days checking machine output, which is dull and leads to careless checking.

Ask three questions for every role affected by a use case:

1. **What does AI take away?** For Northfield's agents: typing routine replies.
2. **What does that free time go to?** Calling back customers with complex problems, following up on quotes, mentoring new agents, improving the knowledge base.
3. **What new skills does the role need?** Judging AI drafts quickly, spotting when the AI is wrong, writing clear feedback, handling the harder cases that remain.

Then update job descriptions, targets and training to match. If the agents' targets still reward only the number of emails closed, they will rush through checking. Northfield changed its team targets to include quality and customer satisfaction alongside volume.

## Building Skills at Three Levels

Not everyone needs the same training.

**Everyone (1 to 2 hours):** the AI use policy; what AI is good and bad at; how to write a clear request; how to check output; real examples from your own company. Repeat a short refresher every six months.

**Regular users in a process (half a day to a day):** the specific tool and workflow; worked examples from their own job; how to give feedback; what to do when the AI is wrong. Then support on the floor during the first weeks.

**Builders and champions (several days, ongoing):** writing and testing AI instructions, connecting tools, measuring results, coaching colleagues. These are the people who will configure and improve AI use cases across the company.

**Leaders (half a day, plus the habit in Chapter 3):** what AI can and can't do, the business case discipline, governance, and leading the change.

## AI Champions

The most effective adoption tool in most companies is a network of AI champions: employees from across the business who use AI well, help colleagues, and feed ideas and problems back to the program.

Good champions are not necessarily the most technical people. They are the ones others already go to for help, who are curious and patient. Northfield started with the 14 regular users from its failed first pilot (Chapter 2), added volunteers, and ended up with 22 champions, at least one in every branch and department.

Give champions:

- Early access to new tools and training.
- A little protected time, such as two hours a week.
- A regular meeting to share what works.
- Recognition: mention their contributions, by name, in leadership updates.

## Getting Adoption in a Pilot

Northfield's pilot (Chapter 17 has the full results) taught the company several things about adoption.

**Put AI where people already work.** The single biggest factor was that drafts appeared automatically in the agents' normal reply box.

**Start with volunteers, then expand.** The pilot began with 12 agents who wanted to try it, then added the rest of the team in week three. The volunteers became the coaches.

**Make feedback visible.** Agents could flag bad drafts with one click. The product owner fixed the most common problems each week and told the team what had changed. Agents saw that their feedback mattered.

**Watch adoption by person and by site.** In week two, adoption was strong in four branches and weak in two. In both weak branches, the team leader was skeptical and had told agents to "use it if you want." A conversation with each team leader, and a day of a champion's time on site, closed most of the gap within a week.

**Celebrate the people, not the tool.** Weekly updates highlighted agents who had found good ways to use the drafts, not just the system's numbers.

> **Tip:** Track adoption from day one, by team and by person, and treat low adoption as a signal to understand, not a reason to blame. The cause is usually something fixable: the tool is awkward, the instructions are wrong, the targets work against it, or a manager is unconvinced.

## Handling Resistance

Some resistance is useful. People who say "this draft is wrong for our trade customers" are improving the system. Treat skeptics as testers, not enemies. Invite them to break the tool and report what they find.

Some resistance is fear. Address it with honest information about jobs and with training that builds confidence.

And some resistance is a sign that the use case isn't good enough yet. If experienced people consistently refuse to use AI output, find out why before you push harder. They may be right.

## Key Points

- Adoption, not technology, decides most AI business cases.
- Answer the job question honestly and early: goals, plans, what you won't do, and how people will be treated.
- Redesign roles and targets, not just tasks, so people aren't left only checking machine output.
- Train at four levels: everyone, process users, builders and champions, and leaders.
- Build a network of AI champions and give them time and recognition.
- Track adoption by team and person from day one, and treat gaps as problems to solve.

## Questions for Your Leadership Team

1. What will we say to employees about AI and jobs, and can every leader say it the same way?
2. Which roles will change most in our first use cases, and how will their targets change?
3. Who are our natural AI champions?
4. How much training time are we really willing to fund?
5. How will we hear honest feedback from skeptics?
