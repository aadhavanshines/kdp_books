# Chapter 3: Your Own AI Habit

You cannot lead well on something you have never done. Leaders who use AI themselves ask better questions, spot vendor exaggeration sooner, and earn more credibility with their teams. Leaders who only watch demos tend to swing between too much enthusiasm and too much fear.

This chapter is about building your own habit: 15 to 30 minutes a day of using an AI assistant for your real work. Within a month, you will have a much clearer sense of where AI helps, where it fails and what your people are dealing with.

## Choose a Tool and Set It Up Properly

Use whichever business-grade assistant your company has approved. Common choices include ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Microsoft Copilot. They differ in details, but any of them is good enough to learn on. If your company has not approved one yet, that is your first action item, because your staff are almost certainly using personal accounts already.

Before you start, check two settings:

- **Data use.** Make sure you are on a business or enterprise plan, or that the setting that allows your conversations to be used for model training is turned off. Ask IT if you are unsure.
- **Custom instructions.** Most assistants let you save standing instructions that apply to every conversation. Use them to tell the assistant who you are and how you like to work.

Here is an example of custom instructions for a leader:

```
I am the chief executive of a regional building-supplies distributor
with 620 employees and 14 branches. I usually want short, direct
answers with the most important point first. When I ask for analysis,
show your assumptions and tell me what you are unsure about. When I
ask for a draft, write in plain, warm, professional English with no
jargon. Push back if my reasoning has a gap.
```

> **Warning:** Never paste confidential information into a tool your company has not approved: customer data, employee records, unannounced results, deal terms or board papers. If in doubt, describe the situation in general terms instead of pasting the document.

## Five Daily Uses for Leaders

Start with these five. They are where most leaders find value first. Appendix B has 30 more prompts for leaders, ready to adapt.

### 1. Thinking Partner

Use the assistant to think through a decision before you discuss it with others. The trick is to ask it to challenge you, not to agree with you.

```
I am considering consolidating two of our branches, which are 12 miles
apart, into one larger site. Here is my reasoning: [your reasoning].
Act as a skeptical board member. What are the five strongest
objections to this plan, and what evidence would I need to answer each
one?
```

### 2. Reading Faster

Summarize long documents you would otherwise skim: industry reports, a consultant's deck, a 40-page contract (if approved for confidential use), a long email thread.

```
Summarize this report for a busy CEO in under 200 words. Then list
the three findings most relevant to a building-supplies distributor,
and one claim in the report that I should be skeptical of, with the
reason.
```

### 3. Writing Faster

Draft the messages that take you longer than they should: a difficult email, a speech outline, a note to the board, feedback to a direct report. Always rewrite the result in your own voice. People notice when a leader's message sounds generated.

### 4. Preparing for Meetings

```
I have a one-to-one tomorrow with our head of sales. Our quote
turnaround has slipped from 20 to 26 hours over six months, and win
rates on quotes are down two points. Suggest five open questions I
could ask to understand what is going on, without sounding as if I
have already decided who is to blame.
```

### 5. Learning a New Area

When you need to get up to speed on something quickly, such as a new regulation, a market or a technology, use the assistant as a patient tutor.

```
Explain the main ideas of AI governance to me as if I were a smart
executive with no technical background. Use examples from a
distribution business. Then give me ten questions to test whether
I've understood it.
```

> **Tip:** Keep a short "AI log" for your first month: what you tried, whether it helped, and how long it saved. At the end of the month you will have real examples to share with your team, and a feel for where AI is strong and weak that no report can give you.

## Learning to Spot Bad Output

As you use AI, train your eye for the ways it goes wrong. Watch for:

- **Confident numbers with no source.** Ask: "Where does that figure come from?" If the answer is vague, treat the number as invented.
- **Smooth but empty writing.** Paragraphs full of words like "transformative," "robust," and "seamless" that say nothing specific. Ask for concrete examples or cut it.
- **Agreement with whatever you said.** Assistants tend to go along with the framing of your question. Ask the same question from the opposite side and compare.
- **Out-of-date facts.** Models are trained on data up to a certain date. For anything recent, ask it to search (if the tool can) or check yourself.
- **Made-up references.** Names of studies, court cases or articles that do not exist. Always check before you repeat them.

Learning these patterns yourself is the best preparation for leading teams who use AI every day. You will know what good checking looks like because you will have done it.

## A Leader's Week With AI

Here is how Dana Reyes, Northfield's CEO, used AI in a typical week after her first month:

| Day | Task | Time saved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Summarized 14 branch reports into one page of issues and trends | About 1 hour |
| Tuesday | Stress-tested a pricing proposal by asking for the strongest objections | Better meeting, not time |
| Wednesday | Drafted a note to all staff about the AI pilot, then rewrote it in her own words | About 30 minutes |
| Thursday | Prepared for a supplier negotiation by asking for likely arguments and counters | About 45 minutes |
| Friday | Turned her rough notes from a board call into a clean summary and action list | About 30 minutes |

Total: roughly three hours saved, and one meeting that went better. None of this shows up in Northfield's accounts. That is fine. The purpose of a leader's personal habit is judgment and credibility, not a return on investment. The return comes from the processes covered in Parts II and III.

## Bringing Your Team Along

Once you have a habit of your own, share it. Leaders set the tone on AI more than any policy does. Three simple moves help:

1. **Show your work.** In a team meeting, show one real example of how you used AI that week, including a mistake it made and how you caught it.
2. **Make it safe to admit using it.** Many employees use AI quietly because they are not sure if it is allowed or worry it looks like cheating. Say clearly what is allowed (Chapter 15 covers a one-page policy).
3. **Ask, don't mandate.** "What's one task you've found AI useful for?" gets better answers than "Everyone must use AI."

## Key Points

- Use AI yourself for 15 to 30 minutes a day on real work. It is the fastest way to build judgment.
- Use an approved, business-grade tool and set custom instructions.
- Start with five uses: thinking partner, reading, writing, meeting preparation and learning.
- Learn to spot bad output: unsourced numbers, empty writing, easy agreement, outdated facts and invented references.
- Share what you learn. Your example shapes your team's habits more than any policy.

## Questions for Your Leadership Team

1. Which AI tool is approved for our use today? If none, how quickly can we approve one?
2. Will each of us commit to a month-long AI log and share results at the end?
3. What would make our staff comfortable telling us how they already use AI?
4. Which kinds of information must never go into an AI tool, even an approved one?
5. What is one recurring task in our own leadership work that AI could help with starting next week?
