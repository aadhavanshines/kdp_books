# Chapter 14: Quick-Starts by Industry

The function chapters apply to almost any business. But leaders often want to know where companies like theirs usually start. This chapter gives a short quick-start for ten common industries: the two or three use cases that most often pay back first, the metric to watch, and the main caution.

Treat these as starting points for your opportunity map (Chapter 4), not as a substitute for it. Your own volumes, data and risks decide your priorities. A use case that is first for most professional services firms may be third for yours.

## Professional Services

Accounting, law, consulting, engineering, architecture and similar firms sell expertise by the hour or by the project. Much of the non-billable time goes into writing, research and admin.

**Start with:**

1. **Proposal and engagement letter drafting** from past proposals and the client's request. Measure hours per proposal and win rate.
2. **Meeting notes and client follow-ups.** Measure non-billable admin hours per professional.
3. **Research and document summarization**, such as first-pass review of client documents, regulations or prior work. Measure turnaround time.

**Watch:** client confidentiality and professional rules. Check what your professional body says about AI, keep client data in approved tools, and make sure a qualified person signs off every piece of advice. Also think about pricing: if AI cuts the hours a job takes, hourly billing may need to move toward fixed fees, or the savings go to the client instead of the firm.

## Manufacturing

Manufacturers have both strong predictive AI use cases on the plant floor and generative AI use cases in the office and around the production line.

**Start with:**

1. **Maintenance support:** predictive maintenance where sensor data exists, and an assistant that answers technicians' questions from manuals and repair history. Measure unplanned downtime.
2. **Quality and incident reports:** AI drafts reports, groups recurring defects by cause, and summarizes trends. Measure time to close quality issues.
3. **Quotes for custom or configured products**, from customer drawings and specifications. Measure quote turnaround.

**Watch:** safety. Anything that touches safety procedures must quote the approved source and stay under engineering control. Also check that drawings and specifications from customers can be shared with your AI vendor under your contracts with them.

## Wholesale and Distribution

Distributors like Northfield run on thin margins, high volumes of orders and constant communication with customers and suppliers.

**Start with:**

1. **Customer email and order-status replies** (Chapter 9). Measure handling time and response time.
2. **Supplier invoice processing** (Chapter 10). Measure cost per invoice.
3. **Product data and catalog content** (Chapter 8). Measure search-to-order conversion and calls to branches.

**Watch:** data quality. Product, pricing and stock data are often spread across systems and inconsistent. Budget for a foundation project to clean them up; it pays off across many use cases.

## Retail and E-Commerce

Retailers have large catalogs, many customers and a lot of repetitive customer contact.

**Start with:**

1. **Product descriptions and content** at scale. Measure conversion on updated pages.
2. **Customer service self-service** for order status, returns and delivery questions, with an easy route to a person. Measure resolution and re-contact, not just deflection.
3. **Review and feedback analysis** to find product and service problems early. Measure returns rate and ratings on affected products.

**Watch:** consumer protection and advertising rules. AI-written product claims must be true. Be clear when customers are talking to an AI assistant, and be careful with any AI-driven pricing for individual customers.

## Healthcare Practices and Clinics

Clinics, dental practices, therapy and veterinary practices face heavy administration and strict privacy rules.

**Start with:**

1. **Administrative communication:** appointment reminders, rescheduling messages, and answers to common non-clinical questions (opening hours, parking, what to bring, billing questions).
2. **Clinical note drafting** using tools designed and approved for clinical use, where a clinician reviews and signs every note. Measure clinician time on documentation.
3. **Billing and coding support** with human review. Measure claim rejection rates.

**Watch:** health data is among the most sensitive data there is, and is heavily regulated. Use only tools that meet the rules where you operate and sign the agreements those rules require. Never let AI give clinical advice to patients without a clinician.

## Financial Services

Banks, insurers, brokers, wealth managers and lenders have strong use cases and some of the strictest regulators.

**Start with:**

1. **Document processing:** applications, claims, identity documents and statements. Measure processing time and error rates.
2. **Adviser and agent support:** summarizing client files and preparing meeting notes, with all advice still given by a qualified person. Measure preparation time.
3. **Complaints handling:** classifying, summarizing and drafting responses for review. Measure time to resolve and regulatory deadlines met.

**Watch:** decisions about credit, insurance pricing or eligibility for individuals are high-risk in many jurisdictions. Involve compliance from day one, keep explainable human decision-making, and expect your regulator to ask how your AI is governed.

## Construction and Trades

Builders, contractors and specialist trades spend a lot of time on estimates, paperwork and coordination.

**Start with:**

1. **Estimates and quotes** from drawings, specifications and site notes. Measure quote turnaround and win rate.
2. **Site reports, daily logs and safety paperwork:** AI drafts from voice notes and photos. Measure admin hours for site managers.
3. **Tender and bid documents:** summarizing large tender packs and drafting responses. Measure bids submitted and hours per bid.

**Watch:** AI can misread drawings and quantities. Every estimate needs a qualified check. Safety documents must reflect what actually happens on site, not a generic template.

## Hospitality and Food Service

Hotels, restaurants, caterers and venues have many small, repetitive communications and thin margins.

**Start with:**

1. **Guest and booking communication:** inquiries, event requests, pre-arrival messages. Measure response time and booking conversion.
2. **Review responses and feedback analysis.** Measure ratings trends.
3. **Staff scheduling and training materials:** draft schedules, onboarding guides and procedures. Measure manager hours and time to train new staff.

**Watch:** allergy and dietary information. Never let AI answer allergy questions from anything except your verified, current menu data, and route uncertain questions to a person.

## Software and Technology Companies

Tech companies often adopt AI fastest, which makes discipline more important, not less.

**Start with:**

1. **AI coding assistants** for developers, with code review and security checks. Measure delivery speed and defect rates, not just lines of code.
2. **Customer support** for product questions from documentation (Chapter 9).
3. **Sales engineering and security questionnaires:** drafting answers to customer security and compliance questionnaires from approved answers. Measure turnaround time.

**Watch:** source code and customer data in AI tools, licensing of AI-generated code, and the temptation to add AI features to the product that customers don't value. Test with customers first.

## Nonprofits and Public Sector Bodies

Charities, associations, schools and public bodies have tight budgets, heavy reporting and a strong duty of trust.

**Start with:**

1. **Grant applications and funder reports:** drafting from program data and past applications. Measure hours per application and funds raised.
2. **Member, donor or citizen inquiries:** answers from approved information, with a clear route to a person. Measure response time.
3. **Summarizing consultations and feedback** from large numbers of responses. Measure time to report.

**Watch:** public trust. Be transparent about AI use, take extra care with vulnerable people's data, and keep humans responsible for every decision that affects people's access to services or support. Public bodies may also face specific rules on automated decision-making and record-keeping.

## What the Ten Have in Common

Look across the ten industries and the same three starting points appear again and again:

1. **Customer or client communication** that is high-volume and routine.
2. **Documents that must be read, extracted or drafted:** invoices, applications, proposals, reports.
3. **Knowledge that staff look up constantly:** policies, procedures, product data, manuals.

If you are unsure where to start, start with one of these three, in the part of your business where the volume is highest.

## Key Points

- Most industries have two or three use cases that usually pay back first; use them to seed your opportunity map.
- The most common starting points are routine communication, document-heavy work, and staff knowledge lookup.
- Each industry has its own main caution: confidentiality, safety, health data, credit decisions, allergy information, public trust.
- Regulated industries should involve compliance from day one.

## Questions for Your Leadership Team

1. Which of the quick-start use cases for our industry match our biggest volumes?
2. What is the main caution for our industry, and who owns it?
3. What are our competitors doing with AI, and what can we learn from it without copying it?
4. Which of the three common starting points is strongest for us?
5. What would our customers expect us to be doing with AI, and what would worry them?
