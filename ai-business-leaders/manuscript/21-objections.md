# Chapter 21: Answering the Skeptics

Every leader who pushes for AI will meet objections. Some come from people who are worried, some from people who have been burned by past technology projects, and some from people who simply see a risk others have missed. The best leaders don't argue skeptics down. They take the objection seriously, give an honest answer, and change the plan when the skeptic is right.

Here are twelve objections you are likely to hear, with honest answers. Use them in leadership meetings, with your board, and with your teams.

## 1. "AI is just hype. It will blow over."

**The honest answer:** Some of it is hype. Many promises about AI agents running whole companies, or AI replacing most office jobs within a year or two, have not come true and may not for a long time. But the core capability, reading and writing language well enough to do real work, is real and already in daily use in millions of businesses. The useful question isn't "Is AI overhyped?" but "Is there a specific use case in our business that pays back today?" Answer that with a measured pilot, not an argument.

## 2. "It makes mistakes. We can't trust it."

**The honest answer:** It does make mistakes. So do people. The question is whether the whole process, AI plus people plus checks, is better than what we do now. Design every use case so that mistakes are caught before they matter: a person reviews customer-facing output, systems do calculations, and quality is sampled regularly. Then measure error rates before and after. At Northfield, errors in customer replies fell slightly with AI drafts, because agents were checking a draft with sources instead of typing from memory.

## 3. "It's too expensive."

**The honest answer:** It can be, if you buy licenses for everyone and change nothing, or fund a big custom project without a business case. It doesn't have to be. Many valuable use cases start with tools you already pay for. Build a full business case (Chapter 6) with every cost included, and fund only what pays back. And remember the other side: not using AI also has a cost if competitors are serving customers faster and cheaper.

## 4. "Our data isn't ready."

**The honest answer:** Often true, and worth taking seriously. But it is rarely true everywhere. Some use cases, such as drafting, summarizing and working from documents people already have, need little data preparation. Start there, and run a foundation project to improve the data that the next use cases need. Use AI as the reason to fix data problems you should have fixed anyway.

## 5. "It will leak our confidential information."

**The honest answer:** It can, if people use unapproved consumer tools or if AI is connected to documents with messy permissions. Both are manageable. Provide an approved business-grade tool with contractual data protection, publish a clear policy on what never goes into AI, and clean up permissions before connecting AI to company documents (Chapter 13). Doing nothing is not safer: your employees are probably already using personal AI accounts for work.

## 6. "It will replace our people, and morale will collapse."

**The honest answer:** AI will change many jobs, and leaders should be honest about that. But in most companies, the first effects show up as slower hiring for growth, less overtime and less tedious work, not as layoffs. How you handle it matters more than the technology: explain the goals, say what you will and won't do, redesign roles deliberately, and train people (Chapter 16). Morale collapses when people hear about changes after they happen, not when leaders are open with them.

## 7. "We tried it and it didn't work."

**The honest answer:** Ask why. Most failed pilots fail for the management reasons in Chapter 2: no baseline, no owner, no process change, no training, no decision rule. Some fail because the use case was wrong or the technology wasn't good enough at the time. Separate those causes. Fix the management problems, re-score the use case, and remember that tools that fell short a year ago may work well now.

## 8. "Let's wait until the technology settles down."

**The honest answer:** It may not settle down for years. Waiting has a cost: your people don't build skills, your data doesn't improve, your governance isn't ready, and competitors who learn now will be faster later. But you don't have to bet big. Small, measured pilots with short contracts let you learn now without locking yourself into today's technology.

## 9. "Our industry is different. AI doesn't apply to us."

**The honest answer:** Every industry has language-heavy, high-volume work: customer communication, documents, knowledge lookup (Chapter 14). The specific use cases differ, and regulated industries need more care, but there are almost always two or three places where AI can help. If, after an honest opportunity map, there really are none, that's a valid conclusion. Reach it with evidence, not by assumption.

## 10. "IT should handle this."

**The honest answer:** IT has a big role: approved tools, security, data access and integration. But if AI belongs only to IT, it will be judged by technical milestones instead of business results. Business leaders must own the use cases and their results, with IT as a partner (Chapter 19). The most successful AI programs are run by business leaders who understand the technology well enough to ask good questions.

## 11. "Regulation is too uncertain."

**The honest answer:** Regulation is evolving, and it differs between countries. But most business use cases, such as drafting, summarizing, answering questions from documents and processing invoices, are low-risk under every framework so far. The higher-risk areas (decisions about hiring, credit, access to services and similar) are well known. Use the risk tiers in Chapter 15, take legal advice on high-risk uses, and track changes. Uncertainty is a reason for care in specific areas, not for doing nothing everywhere.

## 12. "The vendors are just selling us what they have."

**The honest answer:** Often true. That is why you build your own list of use cases first (Chapter 4), score them yourself (Chapter 5), test vendors on your own work rather than their demos (Chapter 7), and keep contracts short. Vendors are useful partners once you know what you want. They are poor guides to what you need.

## When the Skeptic Is Right

Sometimes the objection is correct for your situation. The data really isn't ready for this use case. The risk really is too high for now. The business case really doesn't add up. When that happens, say so clearly and change the plan. Nothing builds credibility for an AI program faster than leaders who stop a project because a skeptic made a good point.

At Northfield, Marcus, the CFO, was the company's most persistent AI skeptic. He also became its most effective one. His insistence on hard benefits, full costs and honest results made the business cases stronger, and when he finally approved funding for the next round of projects, the rest of the leadership team took it as a sign the program was real.

> **Remember:** Your skeptics are your best testers. Invite them in early, give them the numbers, and let them try to break the case. If the case survives, it's stronger. If it doesn't, they've saved you money.

## Key Points

- Take every objection seriously and answer it honestly, with evidence where you can.
- Most objections point to a real risk that can be managed: errors, cost, data, confidentiality, jobs, regulation, vendor bias.
- Doing nothing also carries risks: shadow AI, lost skills and competitors who move first.
- When a skeptic is right, change the plan. It builds credibility.
- Turn your skeptics into testers.

## Questions for Your Leadership Team

1. Which of these twelve objections do we hear most often in our company?
2. Which of them do we, as a leadership team, privately share?
3. Who are our most thoughtful skeptics, and how could we involve them?
4. Has a skeptic ever changed one of our AI plans? If not, are we listening?
5. What evidence would change our own minds about AI, in either direction?
