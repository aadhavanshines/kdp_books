# Chapter 12: HR and People

HR is in an unusual position with AI. It has many good use cases of its own, and it is also responsible for the biggest question AI raises in any company: what happens to people's jobs? This chapter covers HR's own use cases. Chapter 16 covers leading people through the change.

HR is also where the risk of AI is highest in one specific way. Decisions about hiring, pay, promotion and dismissal affect people's lives and are governed by employment and anti-discrimination law. AI used carelessly in these decisions can be unfair, and in a growing number of places it can be illegal. So the rule in HR is clear.

> **Remember:** In HR, AI can help people prepare, draft and answer questions. It must not make or effectively decide employment decisions about individuals without meaningful human judgment, and you must be able to explain any decision it contributed to.

## Use Cases That Pay Back

### 1. Employee Questions About Policies and Benefits

Employees ask HR the same questions constantly: How many vacation days do I have left? What's the parental leave policy? How do I claim mileage? An AI assistant that answers from your policies and handbooks, with links to the source, handles the routine questions and frees HR for the conversations that need a person.

- **Value:** HR time, faster answers for employees.
- **Measure:** routine questions handled without HR, employee satisfaction with the answers, HR hours on routine queries.
- **Watch out:** personal questions (pay, health, disputes, complaints) must go to a person. Make the handoff easy and private.

### 2. Job Descriptions and Job Ads

AI drafts clear, inclusive job descriptions and ads from a hiring manager's notes, and can check existing ones for biased or off-putting language.

- **Value:** hiring manager and recruiter time; sometimes a wider pool of applicants.
- **Measure:** time to post a role; applicant numbers and diversity.

### 3. Interview Preparation

AI can suggest structured interview questions tied to the job's requirements and a scoring guide, which makes interviews fairer and more consistent.

- **Value:** better hiring decisions, more consistent interviews.
- **Measure:** use of structured scorecards; new-hire performance and retention over time.

### 4. Onboarding

AI helps build onboarding plans, answers new starters' questions, and creates role-specific guides from existing documents.

- **Value:** faster time to productivity, better early experience.
- **Measure:** time to competence, 90-day retention.

### 5. Learning and Development

AI tutors and assistants can create practice scenarios, quizzes and short learning materials tailored to a role, and help managers prepare for difficult conversations.

- **Value:** skills, manager quality.
- **Measure:** completion and assessment scores; harder outcomes like engagement and retention over time.

### 6. Workforce Insight

AI can analyze engagement survey comments, exit interview notes and internal feedback to show themes and trends.

- **Value:** better people decisions.
- **Watch out:** aggregate and anonymize. Never use it to identify or monitor individuals' sentiment.

## Use Cases to Approach With Great Care

Some HR uses of AI are legally and ethically sensitive. They are not automatically wrong, but they need legal advice, careful design and human decision-making:

- **Screening or ranking job applicants.** AI systems trained on past hiring decisions can learn past biases. Several jurisdictions now regulate automated hiring tools, requiring bias audits, notices to candidates or human review. Check the rules where you hire before using any tool that filters candidates.
- **Performance evaluation.** AI can help a manager structure and write a review from their own notes. It must not score employees on its own.
- **Monitoring employees.** Tools that track keystrokes, analyze messages or infer mood are legally restricted in many places and corrosive to trust everywhere.
- **Pay and promotion decisions.** AI can help analyze pay equity across groups. It must not set individual pay.

If a vendor tells you their AI can "identify top talent" or "predict flight risk" for individual employees, ask exactly how it works, what data it uses, how it was tested for bias, and what the law says where your employees are. Then ask your employment lawyer.

## Northfield: Starting With Questions, Not Decisions

HR policy questions scored only 11 in Chapter 5 because the volume was modest and the HR knowledge base was out of date. Northfield's HR director decided to use the scoring as a reason to fix the foundation first:

1. HR spent six weeks updating and consolidating the policies into 40 clear documents.
2. They loaded them into the general AI assistant as a shared knowledge source available to all staff, with instructions to answer only from those documents, link the source, and say "Please contact HR" for anything personal or not covered.
3. They tracked which questions the assistant couldn't answer, which showed gaps in the policies themselves.

Within three months, HR estimated that routine policy emails had fallen by about a third. The side benefit was larger: managers now gave consistent answers because they could check the policy in seconds.

The HR director also wrote down what Northfield would not do: no AI screening of applicants, no AI scoring of employees, and no analysis of individual employees' messages. That clear statement, shared with all staff, did more for trust in the AI program than any training session.

## Starter Prompt: Structured Interview Guide

```
Here is the job description for a branch supervisor role: [paste].
Create a structured interview guide with six questions, each tied to
one requirement in the job description. For each question, give:
what it tests, what a strong answer includes, what a weak answer
looks like, and a 1 to 4 scoring scale with a short description of
each level. Use behavioral questions ("Tell me about a time...").
Avoid any question about age, family, health, religion, nationality
or other protected characteristics.
```

## The Traps in HR

- **Automating decisions about people.** Keep humans deciding, and be able to explain every decision.
- **Bias inherited from data.** Past decisions may be biased; AI trained on them will be too.
- **Surveillance by stealth.** It destroys trust faster than any AI benefit can build it.
- **Out-of-date policies.** An AI that answers from wrong policies spreads errors quickly. Fix the documents first.

## Metrics to Track

| Use case | Primary metric | Secondary metric |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Policy questions | Routine queries handled without HR | Employee satisfaction |
| Job ads | Time to post | Applicant pool |
| Interview guides | Use of structured scorecards | New-hire retention |
| Onboarding | Time to competence | 90-day retention |
| Learning | Completion and assessment | Engagement |

## Key Points

- HR has strong, low-risk use cases: policy questions, job ads, interview guides, onboarding and learning.
- Decisions about individuals (hiring, evaluation, pay, dismissal) must stay with people, with legal advice on any AI that touches them.
- Fix and consolidate policies before putting an AI assistant in front of them.
- Say clearly, in writing, what you will not do with AI in HR. It builds trust.

## Questions for Your Leadership Team

1. Which employee questions take most of HR's time, and are our policies current enough for AI to answer them?
2. Are any of our current recruiting or HR tools already using AI to filter or score people? Do we know how?
3. What employment and AI laws apply where we hire?
4. What will we promise employees that we will never do with AI?
5. How could AI help our managers become better managers?
