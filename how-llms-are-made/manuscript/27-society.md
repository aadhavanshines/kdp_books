# Chapter 27: AI and Society: What Comes Next

We've followed a language model from its first token of training data to agents that work for hours, and to models that see, speak, draw and make films. This final chapter steps back to ask what it all means: for work, learning, truth, power and the future. No one knows exactly how this story ends, so this chapter gives you the main questions, the best evidence so far and the arguments on different sides, so you can think about them for yourself.

## The Fastest Adoption in History

ChatGPT reached an estimated 100 million monthly users about two months after its launch in November 2022, faster than any consumer app before it. By October 2025, OpenAI said it had more than 800 million weekly users. Add Gemini, Claude, Meta AI, Doubao, DeepSeek, Copilot and the AI built into phones, search engines and office software, and a large share of humanity now uses language models regularly, a few years after almost nobody had heard of them.

## Work and Jobs

The biggest economic question is what AI will do to work.

- **Productivity gains are real in some tasks.** In a 2023 study of more than 5,000 customer-support agents, **Erik Brynjolfsson**, **Danielle Li** and **Lindsey Raymond** found that an AI assistant raised the number of problems resolved per hour by about 14% on average, and by about 34% for novice workers, who effectively learned from the best workers' patterns built into the tool. Studies of programmers, consultants and writers have found similar gains on suitable tasks, along with mistakes when people trusted the AI outside its competence.
- **Early signs of disruption.** In August 2025, Brynjolfsson, **Bharat Chandar** and **Ruyu Chen** at Stanford, using payroll data from millions of American workers, found that employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in the occupations most exposed to AI, such as software development and customer service, had fallen about 13% relative to other groups since late 2022, mainly through less hiring, while older workers in the same jobs were unaffected. An updated version in 2026 found the gap had widened, though it found no sign yet of economy-wide job losses.
- **Historical perspective.** Past technologies, from the power loom to the spreadsheet, eliminated some jobs, transformed many and created new ones, but the transitions were often painful for those affected. Whether AI will follow that pattern, or be different because it can do so many cognitive tasks, is the central debate.

## Learning and Education

AI can be the most patient tutor in the world: explaining a concept ten different ways, at any hour, in any language. Early studies of AI tutoring, including a 2024 randomized trial in Nigeria run with the World Bank, showed substantial learning gains. But AI can also do students' homework for them, and essays can no longer be assumed to be a student's own work. Schools and universities are redesigning assignments and assessments, and teaching students to use AI as a thinking partner rather than a replacement for thinking.

## Truth, Trust and Hallucination

Language models can produce fluent, confident text that's false (Chapter 1).

> **Did You Know?** In 2023, two New York lawyers submitted a legal brief in a case against the airline Avianca that cited six court decisions that didn't exist. They had been invented by ChatGPT, which, when asked, assured them the cases were real. The judge fined them $5,000. Since then, researchers tracking such incidents have recorded hundreds of court filings around the world containing AI-invented citations.

Beyond accidental errors, AI makes it cheap to produce persuasive text, images, voices and videos at scale, which raises concerns about disinformation, scams and "AI slop", low-quality generated content flooding the web and social media. Responses include watermarking and content credentials (Chapter 25), platform rules, media literacy, and AI systems that cite sources so claims can be checked.

## Companions and Mental Health

Millions of people talk to AI for company, advice and emotional support. For some, it's a genuine help: someone to talk to at 3 a.m., practice for difficult conversations, or a first step toward seeking help. But there are serious concerns, especially for young and vulnerable people: dependence, chatbots that validate harmful beliefs (the sycophancy problem of Chapter 13), and tragic cases that have led to lawsuits and new safety measures. AI companies have added protections such as age-appropriate settings, crisis resources and detection of distress, and several governments are writing rules for AI companions.

## Bias and Fairness

Models learn from human text, including its prejudices (Chapter 4). They can perform worse in some languages and dialects, reproduce stereotypes, or be used to make unfair decisions about jobs, loans or policing. In 2021, **Emily Bender**, **Timnit Gebru**, **Angelina McMillan-Major** and **Margaret Mitchell** warned in "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" that ever-larger models trained on vast, unexamined web data would encode its biases, and that their size made this hard to audit. Their paper, and the controversy around Gebru's departure from Google, made these concerns central to the field. Measuring and reducing bias is now standard practice, but far from solved.

## Power and Money

Building frontier models requires chips, data centers and billions of dollars (Part III), so they're built by a handful of companies and countries. This raises questions about concentration of power: who decides how these systems behave, who profits, and who gets left out. Open models (Chapter 21), public research and regulation are the main counterweights.

There's also a financial question. The world's largest companies are spending hundreds of billions of dollars a year on AI infrastructure. Optimists point to rapidly growing revenues and real productivity gains; skeptics compare it to the railway mania of the 1840s or the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s, booms that built lasting infrastructure but bankrupted many investors along the way. Both could be partly right.

## The Long-Term Question

Some of the people who built this technology believe that AI may soon match or exceed human abilities across most intellectual work, sometimes called **artificial general intelligence (AGI)**. Some think this could happen within years; others think it's decades away, or that today's methods will hit fundamental limits. Some, including Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, two of the "godfathers" of deep learning, have warned that sufficiently advanced AI could pose risks on the scale of pandemics or nuclear war, and in May 2023 hundreds of AI researchers and executives signed a one-sentence statement saying that mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority. Others, including Yann LeCun, the third "godfather", consider such fears overblown and argue that today's language models lack essential ingredients of intelligence, such as a real understanding of the physical world.

What's not in dispute is that capabilities have grown faster than almost anyone predicted, and that the decisions being made now, about safety, openness, regulation and who benefits, will matter for a long time.

## What Comes Next

A few trends seem likely to continue:

- **Agents that work longer and more independently**, on more complex tasks, from software to science.
- **AI for science**: models that help design drugs and materials, prove theorems and run experiments, following AlphaFold's path.
- **Everything multimodal**, with models that see, hear, speak, draw and act, including in robots.
- **More efficient models**, running on phones and laptops, and cheaper per answer every year.
- **More regulation, more lawsuits and more public debate**, as society decides what it wants from this technology.

## A Closing Thought

At its heart, a large language model is a strange and wonderful thing: a mathematical structure, built from the collected writing of humanity, that learned to continue our sentences so well that it learned to explain, reason, code and create along the way. It is made by thousands of people, from the researchers who invented its pieces, credited throughout this book, to the data workers, chipmakers, engineers and power-plant operators behind every answer. It's neither magic nor "just autocomplete". Understanding how it's made, from the data to the chips to the training and beyond, is the best way to use it wisely, question it intelligently, and help decide what it should become.

## Key Takeaways

- Language models were adopted faster than any technology before them.
- Studies show real productivity gains, especially for less experienced workers, and early signs of reduced hiring of young workers in AI-exposed jobs.
- AI can be a powerful tutor and a shortcut around learning; hallucination and cheap fake content challenge trust.
- Companionship, bias, concentration of power and a possible investment bubble are major concerns.
- Experts disagree sharply on how soon AI might match human abilities and how risky that would be; the decisions made now will matter.
