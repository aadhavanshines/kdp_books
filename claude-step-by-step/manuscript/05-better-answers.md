# Chapter 5: Turning a Good Answer into a Finished One

Even with the Finished-Work Formula, the first answer is sometimes 90 percent right. This chapter gives you the quickest ways to close the last 10 percent, so you never have to rewrite anything by hand unless you want to.

## The Three-Second Diagnosis

When an answer isn't right, ask yourself which of these is the problem. Each one has a different fix.

| What's wrong | Usual cause | Fastest fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generic, full of blanks or placeholders | Missing raw material | Give the facts: "Here are the details: ..." |
| Wrong tone or length | Missing reader or rules | "Shorter and warmer; it's for a nervous first-time customer" |
| A fact or number is wrong | Claude guessed | Give the correct fact, or turn on web search |
| Right content, wrong shape | Format not stated | "Put this in a table with columns for..." |
| Misunderstood the task | Ambiguous request | Edit your message (Chapter 3) and say it more clearly |
| Just not good | Bad luck | Retry once; if still bad, start a new chat with a better request |

## Edit Claude's Draft Directly

Sometimes you only want to change one sentence. Typing "change the third sentence to..." works, but there is a faster way. When Claude produces a draft, such as an email card or a document, you can click into the draft and **edit it in place**, like a normal document. Change a word, delete a line, and then click Copy or Send. Your edits stay in the draft.

Use in-place editing for small changes you already know how to make. Use a follow-up message when you want Claude to do the work, for example "make the whole thing more formal."

## Twelve Follow-Ups That Fix Almost Anything

Keep this list handy. Each one is a complete message.

1. "Cut it by half without losing any facts."
2. "Make it sound like a person wrote it: shorter sentences, no clichés."
3. "Start with the most important point."
4. "Put this in a table."
5. "Give me three different versions: formal, friendly and very short."
6. "What's missing? Ask me for anything you need, then rewrite."
7. "List every fact or number you're not certain about."
8. "Read it as the recipient. What would confuse or annoy them? Then fix it."
9. "Keep everything else the same, but change only the second paragraph."
10. "Use simpler words. My reader isn't a native English speaker."
11. "Add a clear next step and a deadline at the end."
12. "Now check this against my original request and tell me anything you missed."

The last one is underrated. Asking Claude to check its own work against your request catches surprising things: a forgotten detail, a word limit exceeded, a name spelled two ways.

## Ask for Options, Then Choose

When you don't know exactly what you want, ask for several options instead of one answer.

```prompt
Give me five subject lines for this email, from most formal to most casual. Mark the one you'd pick and say why in one line.
```

Choosing between options is much faster than describing what you want in the abstract. It also teaches you what you like, which makes your next request better.

## Let Claude Criticize You

Claude is polite by default. If you want an honest opinion, ask for it directly:

```prompt
Here is my cover letter. Be a tough, experienced hiring manager. Give me the five biggest weaknesses, most serious first, and then show me a better version of the weakest paragraph.
```

You'll get far more useful feedback than "This is a great letter!"

## When to Start Over

Follow-ups work best for small changes. If you've sent four or five corrections and the answer is still drifting, start a **new chat**. Paste your best version of the request, including what you learned from the failed attempt ("Don't mention pricing; keep it under 100 words"). A fresh start almost always beats a long, tangled conversation.

## When Claude Says No or Hedges

Occasionally Claude declines a request or adds a lot of cautions. Usually this happens because the request looked risky out of context. Add the context:

- Not this: "How do I get into a locked car?"
- But this: "I locked my keys in my own car, a 2019 Honda Civic. What are my options, and who should I call?"

If Claude is overly cautious about medical, legal or money questions, say what you'll do with the answer: "I'm preparing questions for my doctor's appointment on Tuesday; I won't act without her advice." Claude will still be careful, but much more useful.

## Exercise

Take any answer from your last few chats that you weren't fully happy with. Diagnose the problem with the table at the top of this chapter, then fix it with one follow-up from the list of twelve. How many messages did it take to reach "finished"?

## Key Points

- Diagnose first: missing facts, missing reader or rules, a wrong fact, or the wrong shape.
- Edit drafts in place for small changes; use follow-ups for bigger ones.
- Ask for options, ask for honest criticism, and ask Claude to check its own work.
- After four or five corrections, start a new chat with a better request.
- When Claude hedges, add context about who you are and why you're asking.
